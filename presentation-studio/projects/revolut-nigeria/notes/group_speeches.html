Slide 1 · Revolut in Nigeria

Good morning everyone. Today we look at one question: if Revolut wanted to enter Nigeria, how should it adapt to Nigerian culture?

First, one important point. This is a hypothetical case. Revolut is not available in Nigeria today. On Revolut's own website, Nigeria is not on the list of countries where you can open an account. So this is our idea, not a real Revolut plan.

To answer the question, we use the Trompenaars model from our course. First we look at the market, then the culture. Then we compare stereotypes with reality, and we finish with our recommendations and a campaign idea.

Slide 2 · The market

So, what does the Nigerian market look like? The first thing to know is that Nigeria is already very digital.

In 2024, Nigeria's instant bank transfer system handled close to 11 billion transactions. That's more than double the 2022 figure, according to the Central Bank of Nigeria. Also, 64% of adults now use digital financial services, and mobile money use went from 12% to 38% in just three years.

So the message is simple: Nigeria is not an empty market. There is already strong competition. To succeed, Revolut would have to win people's trust, be reliable and adapt to local needs.

ClaraSlides 3–4

Slide 3 · The seven dimensions

Thank you, Farhan. To understand culture in business, we use the model of Trompenaars and Hampden-Turner. It has seven dimensions, and you can see all of them here.

Our course asks us to pick the three or four most important ones for our market. We chose four: universalism versus particularism, individualism versus collectivism, specific versus diffuse, and sequential versus synchronous time.

Why these four? Because for a bank they connect to what matters most: trust, the influence of other people, business relationships, and fast, reliable service.

One warning: this model shows general tendencies. It doesn't describe every person. So we treat each point as an idea to test, not as a fact.

Slide 4 · Universalism vs particularism

The first dimension asks a simple question: what comes first, the rules or the relationship? Universalism means the same rules for everyone. Particularism means relationships and reputation matter more.

For a bank, both matter. The rules never change: every customer must pass the same identity checks. But people may also choose a bank because of its reputation, because someone recommended it, or because they can easily get help.

So our advice is to have one clear process for everyone, plus good customer support and trusted local partners.

For example, if a friend recommends you, you still do the same checks as everyone else. The relationship opens the door, but the rule protects everyone.

Slide 5 · Individualism vs collectivism

Thank you, Clara. The second dimension asks: do people decide alone, or does the group influence them?

When it comes to money, people may decide for themselves, but family, friends or other traders can influence them. Many people first hear about a new app from someone they trust.

But we shouldn't assume this is true for everyone. A young professional in Lagos and a trader in a small town can behave very differently.

So we suggest that Revolut tests referral programmes, uses real customer stories only with permission, and makes simple content that people want to share.

For example, a trader hears about the app from another trader, but still compares the fees before deciding.

Slide 6 · Specific vs diffuse

The third dimension asks: is a business relationship only about the job, or does it go further? In a specific culture, business is just business. In a diffuse culture, people want to know and trust you before they work with you.

This matters most for Revolut's future partners in Nigeria, like banks and payment companies. They will look at the offer, but also at the people behind it. Are they reliable? Will they still be here in two years?

So Revolut should build long-term relationships and keep the same contact people. But it should also be clear and put every agreement in writing. Good relationships and clear contracts work together.

Slide 7 · Sequential vs synchronous time

Thank you, Joanes. The fourth dimension is about time. Sequential means one task at a time, on a fixed schedule. Synchronous means doing several things at once, with more flexibility.

Here we need to be careful. It's easy to say "time is relaxed in Nigeria", but that's a stereotype. For payments, it's the opposite: Nigeria has had instant bank transfers across the country since 2011, so people expect their money to move in seconds.

Meeting styles can be different from company to company, but the service must be fast and reliable. Our advice is reliable transfers, a clear message when there is a delay, clear support times, and a simple way to fix problems.

For example, if a transfer fails, the customer needs to see what happened and how it will be fixed.

Slide 8 · Stereotype vs reality

This slide is the heart of our analysis: stereotypes versus reality.

Some people say Nigerians don't trust digital banking. In fact, 64% of adults already use digital financial services. What really builds trust is protection against fraud and reliable service.

Some say everyone prefers cash. Cash is still important, but mobile money use has tripled in three years.

Some say everyone has the same needs. That's not true. For example, about three in four people without access to financial services live in the North West and North East.

And some say businesses are relaxed about deadlines. Yet instant payments have been normal since 2011.

So culture is only one factor. Age, income, education, location, digital experience and the type of company all change how people behave.

Slide 9 · Recommendations

Thank you, Oman. So what should Revolut actually do? Our main idea is on the slide: change the way you build relationships, but never lower your standards.

With customers, Revolut should use simple language, show fees and exchange rates clearly, show the status of every transfer, and make help easy to reach.

With partners, it should send formal written proposals, but also take time to build the relationship, follow up regularly and write down every agreement.

Inside the company, everyone needs clear roles and deadlines and a clear way to escalate problems. And the company should listen to the local team, because they know the market best.

Slide 11 · Market-entry plan

Thank you, Samuel. So how should Revolut enter Nigeria? Step by step, in five stages.

First, Revolut would check the rules. It would need local legal advice to choose the right licence or partnership, and we don't assume any licence. Second, it would find local partners, like banks and payment companies. Third, it would adapt the product, with payments in naira, local payment systems, support people can reach, and clear prices.

Fourth, it would start with a small test: one city, one customer group, clear goals. Lagos is the obvious place to start, but Lagos is not all of Nigeria. Fifth, it would grow only when the results show it works.

In short: build trust first, then grow.

Slide 12 · Conclusion

To finish, four simple principles. Rules means following the law and being transparent. Relationships means building trust with customers and partners. Community means understanding how people influence each other. And operations means being reliable and communicating clearly.

Our final message: Revolut's success in Nigeria would not depend only on bringing a global product to a new market. It would depend on adapting that product, and its relationships, to the reality of the local market.
