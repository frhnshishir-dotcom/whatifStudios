## 01 · REVOLUT IN NIGERIA
*cover · ~62 s · 135 words*

Good morning. Our question is simple to ask and hard to answer: how should Revolut adapt its business relationships, communication, marketing and operations to enter Nigeria successfully?

Two things to be clear about from the start. First, this is a hypothetical case study. Revolut does not currently list Nigeria among the countries where you can open a personal account, so nothing here is an announcement or a description of a real Revolut product. Second, we use the course framework, Trompenaars and Hampden-Turner's seven dimensions, as a lens, not as proof of how any individual behaves.

Our structure follows the course method: market context, cultural profiling, a critical look at stereotypes versus reality, and then concrete recommendations, including a proposed campaign.

[Source: Revolut Help Centre, 'Where you can sign up for Revolut', accessed 24 Nov 2026.]

## 02 · Nigeria has a rapidly evolving digital payments ecosystem
*stats · ~77 s · 167 words*

Before any cultural analysis, the market context. Nigeria is not a blank space waiting for digital banking.

According to the Central Bank of Nigeria's 2025 fintech report, close to 11 billion transactions went through the NIBSS Instant Payment platform in 2024, up from about 5 billion in 2022. Real-time interbank transfers have existed nationally since 2011.

On the customer side, EFInA's Access to Financial Services survey, fielded in April and May 2026, finds that 64 percent of adults use digital financial services, and mobile money use more than tripled, from 12 to 38 percent of adults since 2023.

A note on method: the brief also mentioned total e-payment values for 2025. Published figures disagree on definitions and periods, and I could not verify them in the original CBN document, so I left them out rather than mix metrics.

The implication: a competitive, developed ecosystem. Revolut would need to differentiate through trust, reliability, relevance and local adaptation.

[Sources: CBN (2025) pp. 5, 9; EFInA A2F 2026 key highlights.]

## 03 · Understanding the cultural context behind business decisions
*dimensions · ~78 s · 169 words*

Here are the seven dimensions as named in our course. Each one is paired with the course's profiling question.

The course asks us to select three to four dominant dimensions for the market. We chose four, highlighted, because they map directly onto what makes or breaks a fintech: trust in rules versus relationships; how social influence shapes financial decisions; how business partnerships are built; and what people expect in terms of time and reliability.

The other three still matter. For example, assigned versus acquired status may affect how a foreign company presents its leadership to Nigerian partners. But for a digital financial service they are less central to the entry decision.

One important caveat. I did not use national scores. I found no current, reliable scores for Nigeria I could verify, and in any case the framework describes tendencies, not individuals. So every point on the next slides is phrased as a question to investigate, then as a recommendation.

[Sources: Pouchelet, course slides pp. 24–34; Trompenaars & Hampden-Turner, 1997.]

## 04 · Rules create credibility. Relationships help build trust
*dimension · ~70 s · 152 words*

Universalism versus particularism asks: do rules or relationships come first?

In fintech, both matter at once. Rules are non-negotiable: identity checks, Know Your Customer requirements and regulation apply to every customer. The CBN's own report links Nigeria's digital finance growth to integrity, KYC and fraud prevention. At the same time, the course reminds us that in more particularist settings, trust often travels through relationships and reputation.

So our hypothesis, to be validated with local research, is that customers judge a new provider both on formal safeguards and on reputation and reachable support.

The recommendation keeps the rule universal and builds the relationship around it: one transparent onboarding standard, support people can actually reach, and credibility through trusted local partners.

The example makes the line clear: a referral can bring a customer to the door, but it never replaces verification.

[Sources: course slides pp. 24, 32; CBN (2025) on KYC and fraud prevention.]

## 05 · Financial decisions can be personal, but social influence matters
*dimension · ~71 s · 153 words*

Individualism versus collectivism asks whether a decision is aimed at personal or group benefit. Trompenaars originally calls the group pole 'communitarianism'; our course uses 'collectivism'.

For a financial service, this becomes a question about discovery and trust. Who influences the decision to try a new app? It could be family, colleagues, a merchant association, or nobody at all.

We should not assume the answer. A young professional in Lagos and a trader in a smaller town may behave very differently. That is why we frame it as a hypothesis and recommend testing.

The recommendations follow: test referral strategies, use real testimonials only with permission, create educational content people want to share, and segment by actual behaviour.

The example shows both poles at once: the merchant hears about the service through their community, but still makes an individual decision based on fees and reliability.

[Sources: course slides pp. 25, 32; Trompenaars & Hampden-Turner, 1997.]

## 06 · Business relationships can extend beyond the transaction
*dimension · ~63 s · 136 words*

Specific versus diffuse is about how far a business relationship extends. Is it strictly professional, or does it involve a broader personal bond? The course's example is the need for business dinners and personal connection in more diffuse cultures.

For Revolut this matters most in the B2B side of market entry: banks, payment providers and distribution partners. The hypothesis is that these partners may judge not only the technical proposal, but also the people behind it and whether they will still be there in two years.

The recommendations combine both poles. Invest in long-term local relationships and keep the same contacts over time, which is the diffuse side. But also state expectations clearly and document every agreement, which is the specific side. Good relationships and clear contracts reinforce each other.

[Sources: course slides pp. 26, 33.]

## 07 · Cultural expectations and operational speed are not the same thing
*dimension · ~70 s · 152 words*

Sequential versus synchronous time asks whether people do one thing at a time on a fixed schedule, or several things in parallel with flexible coordination.

Here we need to be careful. It would be easy, and wrong, to slide into the stereotype that time is 'relaxed' in Nigeria. The evidence points the other way for payments: the CBN notes that Nigeria implemented nationwide real-time interbank payments in 2011, well ahead of many advanced economies. Customers already expect money to move instantly.

So we separate two things. Meeting and negotiation styles may vary between organisations and are worth observing. Operational speed, though, is not negotiable: reliable processing, honest delay notifications, defined support targets, a clear dispute process, and coordination between the Lagos team and global teams.

The example: if a transfer fails, the customer needs a clear status and a resolution, full stop.

[Sources: CBN (2025) p. 5; course slides pp. 27, 34.]

## 08 · Cultural tendencies should never become assumptions about every customer
*myth-reality · ~76 s · 164 words*

This is the critical heart of the analysis. The course asks us to show how factors like age, social class, the B2B or B2C context and company culture moderate any national tendency.

Take each stereotype in turn. 'Nigerians distrust digital banking': EFInA's 2026 survey finds 64 percent of adults already use digital financial services. What decides trust is fraud protection and reliability; the CBN itself calls fraud an enduring reputational burden.

'Everyone prefers cash': cash still matters where income is earned in cash, but mobile money use more than tripled in three years.

'Everyone has the same needs': financial exclusion is concentrated. About three-quarters of excluded adults live in the North West and North East.

And 'relaxed about deadlines' ignores the fact that instant payments have been standard since 2011.

The moderating factors at the bottom are the real segmentation variables. Culture is one input among them.

[Sources: EFInA A2F 2026 highlights and 9 Oct 2026 release; CBN (2025) pp. 5–6; course pp. 35–36.]

## 09 · Adapt the way we build relationships, not the standards we promise
*features · ~68 s · 147 words*

Now the operational rules of action, which is the last step of the course method.

The course positions Sub-Saharan Africa in an intermediate communication context: status matters, debate can be explicit, and the relationship counts. Using the course's four levers for adapting a message, which are tone, structure, salutations and call to action, we adapt how we communicate, not what we promise.

For customers: plain language, fees and exchange rates shown before confirmation, a visible transaction status and support people can reach.

For partners: formal written proposals, combined with real investment in the relationship, regular follow-up and every commitment documented. This addresses both the particularist and the diffuse dimensions.

Inside the company: clear owners, agreed deadlines, a defined escalation path, and genuine respect for the local team's market knowledge, so that headquarters does not overrule people who understand the market better.

[Source: course slides pp. 39, 50–52.]

## 10 · YOUR MONEY. YOUR MOVE.
*campaign · ~68 s · 148 words*

This is the proposed campaign: 'Your money. Your move.' To be clear, it is our concept for a hypothetical locally adapted campaign, not a Revolut advertisement.

[Play the 30-second film.]

Why this concept? It follows directly from the analysis. Instead of one 'average Nigerian', it shows three distinct people: an entrepreneur, a professional and a student. That is our stereotype-versus-reality point, turned into creative.

It is built to be shared and recognised within people's own networks, which speaks to the collectivist hypothesis. It shows transparency rather than claiming it: fees, rates and transaction status are visible in the interface, which is how we earn trust in a particularist setting while keeping universal rules. And its language of ambition and control speaks to individual goals.

On production: the people shots are AI-generated video, and the interface is a conceptual mock-up with illustrative data. No real Revolut features are shown.

## 11 · Build local credibility before scaling
*timeline · ~60 s · 131 words*

Putting it together, we recommend a five-stage entry that builds credibility before scale.

First, a regulatory assessment. Revolut does not currently offer its standard account in Nigeria, and the right route, whether a licence, a partnership or another model, depends on the product and needs local legal advice. We deliberately do not assume any licence.

Second, local partnerships with banks, payment providers and distributors, built with the long-term relationship approach from the specific-versus-diffuse analysis.

Third, localise the product: naira flows, integration with local payment rails, reachable support, and transparent pricing.

Fourth, a controlled pilot: one segment, one city, clear metrics. Lagos is the obvious candidate, but the stereotype analysis warns us that Lagos is not all of Nigeria.

Fifth, expand only on evidence.

[Source: Revolut Help Centre, accessed 24 Nov 2026.]

## 12 · Successful market entry starts with understanding people
*principles · ~56 s · 121 words*

To conclude, four principles summarise the analysis.

Rules: compliance and transparency are universal and non-negotiable.

Relationships: trust is built with customers and partners over time.

Community: social influence matters, so understand it and test it rather than assume it.

Operations: reliable service and clear communication are where trust is ultimately won or lost.

Our final message: Revolut's opportunity in Nigeria would not depend only on bringing a global product to a new market. It would depend on adapting that product, and its business relationships, to the realities of the local market. And those realities differ by person, place and segment, which is exactly why cultural frameworks are a starting point, not a conclusion.

Thank you. We're happy to take your questions.
